Let \(M\) be any maximal matching (no edge can be added to it while keeping it a matching) and let \(N\) be a maximum matching (largest possible). We must show \(|M|\ge\tfrac12|N|\), so that \(M\) is never worse than half the optimum — a \(2\)-approximation.

  1. Every edge of \(N\) is blocked by \(M\). Take any edge \(e'\in N\). At least one of its two endpoints must already be an endpoint of some edge \(e\in M\). Otherwise both endpoints of \(e'\) would be untouched by \(M\), so \(e'\) shares no vertex with any edge of \(M\), and \(M\cup\{e'\}\) would be a matching strictly larger than \(M\) — contradicting maximality of \(M\).
  2. Charge each \(N\)-edge to a matched vertex. Map each edge \(e'\in N\) to one of its endpoints that lies in \(V(M)\), the set of vertices touched by \(M\). Since \(M\) has \(|M|\) edges, \(|V(M)|\le 2|M|\).
  3. This map is injective. Because \(N\) is a matching, its edges are pairwise vertex-disjoint, so no vertex is an endpoint of two different edges of \(N\). Hence distinct edges of \(N\) map to distinct vertices of \(V(M)\).
  4. Count. An injection from the \(|N|\) edges of \(N\) into \(V(M)\) gives \(|N|\le|V(M)|\le 2|M|\), so \(|M|\ge \tfrac12|N|\).

Combined with the trivial \(|M|\le|N|\) (since \(N\) is maximum), the size of any maximal matching lies within a factor \(2\) of the maximum, which is the claimed \(2\)-approximation.
