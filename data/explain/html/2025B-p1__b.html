The statement is true. The trick is to reduce the rectangular product to several square products and then invoke fast matrix multiplication.

  1. Let \(A\) be \(n\times n\) and \(B\) be \(n\times n^2\). Cut \(B\) vertically into \(n\) square blocks, each \(n\times n\): \(B=[\,B_1\mid B_2\mid\cdots\mid B_n\,]\).
  2. Matrix multiplication distributes over this block structure column-block by column-block: \(AB=[\,AB_1\mid AB_2\mid\cdots\mid AB_n\,]\). So computing \(AB\) reduces to computing the \(n\) square products \(AB_1,\dots,AB_n\).
  3. Each \(AB_i\) is a product of two \(n\times n\) matrices, which fast matrix multiplication does in \(O(n^{\omega})\) operations, where \(\omega<3\) is the matrix-multiplication exponent (known to be roughly \(2.37\)).
  4. There are \(n\) such products, so the total cost is \(n\cdot n^{\omega}=n^{\omega+1}\).
Because \(\omega<3\), we get \(\omega+1<4\), hence \(n^{\omega+1}not strictly less than \(n^4\). It is exactly the sub-cubic exponent \(\omega<3\) that pushes us below \(n^4\).
