Claim with weights drawn uniformly from \(\{1,\dots,p(n)\}\) for \(p(n)=10n^5\), with probability at least \(0.9\) every pair \(u,v\) has a unique minimum-weight path.

Strategy handle one pair at a time: bound the chance a single pair has a tie using the isolation lemma, then union-bound over all pairs.

Key idea the isolation lemma says that if you give items random weights from a large enough range, the minimum-weight subset out of any family of subsets (here, the lightest path among all \(u\)-\(v\) paths) comes out unique with high probability — and the failure chance shrinks as the range grows.

Why this move "unique minimum" over a possibly huge family of objects (there can be exponentially many paths) is exactly what the isolation lemma is built for: its bound depends only on the number of edges, not on how many paths there are.

  1. Set the range. Pick \(p(n)=10n^5\), and give every edge an independent uniform weight from \(\{1,\dots,p(n)\}\).
  2. Fix one pair. For vertices \(u,v\), let \(\mathcal{F}_{u,v}\) be the family of all \(u\)-\(v\) paths, each viewed as its set of edges. The number of edges is \(m\le\binom{n}{2}\le n^2\).
  3. Apply the isolation lemma. With random weights from a range of size \(p(n)\) on \(m\) elements, the minimum-weight member of \(\mathcal{F}_{u,v}\) is unique except with probability at most \(\dfrac{m}{p(n)}\le\dfrac{n^2}{10n^5}=\dfrac{1}{10n^3}\). Call the pair bad if its lightest path is not unique.
  4. Union bound over pairs. There are \(\binom{n}{2}\le n^2\) pairs, so the chance that some pair is bad is at most \(\binom{n}{2}\cdot\dfrac{1}{10n^3}\le\dfrac{1}{10n}\le\dfrac{1}{10}\).
  5. Read off the guarantee. Hence with probability at least \(1-\dfrac{1}{10}=0.9\) no pair is bad — every pair has a single lightest path, as required.

The whole trick is that one random weighting quietly breaks all the ties at once: each pair is safe with room to spare, and even after adding up the tiny failure chances over every pair we stay comfortably above \(0.9\).
