Claim False: a symmetric matrix \(A\) with \(\det(A)\ge 0\) need not be PSD.

Strategy Give one symmetric matrix with nonnegative determinant that is clearly not PSD.

Key idea The determinant is the product of eigenvalues, so an even number of negative eigenvalues keeps it positive while the matrix is far from PSD.

Why this move A claimed implication is fastest to kill with a counterexample, and "determinant vs. eigenvalues" points straight at a matrix with two negative eigenvalues.

  1. Pick the matrix. Let \(A=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\ 0&-1\end{bmatrix}=-I\), which is symmetric.
  2. Check the hypothesis. \(\det(A)=(-1)(-1)-0=1\ge 0\), so the assumption holds.
  3. Fail PSD. Its eigenvalues are both \(-1\), and \(x^\top(-I)x=-\lVert x\rVert^2<0\) for any \(x\neq 0\), so \(A\) is not PSD (in fact negative definite).

The determinant only sees the product of eigenvalues, so two negatives cancel in the sign and hide a matrix that is the opposite of PSD.
