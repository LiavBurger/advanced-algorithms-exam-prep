Goal Give an efficient randomized test deciding whether two branching programs \(A_1\cdots A_m\) and \(B_1\cdots B_m\) compute the same polynomial (their \((1,1)\)-entry), with error below \(0.1\).

Strategy Bound the degree of each polynomial, then plug random values into both and compare, using Schwartz-Zippel to control the error.

Key idea Two polynomials are equal iff their difference is identically zero, and a nonzero low-degree polynomial rarely vanishes at a random point.

Why this move Equality of polynomials given as products is exactly polynomial identity testing, and Schwartz-Zippel is the go-to tool once you know a degree bound.

  1. Degree bound. Each matrix entry has degree at most \(1\), and each matrix multiplication adds at most \(1\) to the degree, so after \(m\) products the \((1,1)\)-entries \(P_A\) and \(P_B\), and their difference \(Q=P_A-P_B\), have degree at most \(m\).
  2. The test. Assign each variable \(x_i\) an independent uniform value from \(S=\{1,\dots,100m\}\), compute both matrix products numerically, and answer "equal" iff the two \((1,1)\)-entries match.
  3. Error analysis. If the programs are equal, \(Q\equiv 0\) and the entries always match, so the test never errs. If they differ, \(Q\neq 0\) has degree \(\le m\), and by Schwartz-Zippel \(\Pr[Q=0]\le \dfrac{m}{100m}=\dfrac{1}{100}<0.1\).

Each run is just \(m\) multiplications of \(3\times 3\) matrices, so it is fast, and a random point almost never fools a nonzero polynomial, keeping the error well under \(0.1\).
