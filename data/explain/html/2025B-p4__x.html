The idea is to move to point-value form at the \(n\)-th roots of unity, where dividing polynomials is trivial. The algorithm is:

  1. Evaluate \(f\) and \(g\) at the \(n\)-th roots of unity \(1,\omega,\omega^2,\dots,\omega^{n-1}\).
  2. For each \(i=0,\dots,n-1\), compute \(f(\omega^i)/g(\omega^i)\).
  3. Interpolate the unique polynomial \(h\) of degree \(\deg f-\deg g\) whose value at \(\omega^i\) equals \(f(\omega^i)/g(\omega^i)\).

Correctness. By assumption \(g(\omega^i)\neq 0\) for every \(i\), so every division in step 2 is well-defined. Since \(f=g\cdot h\), we have \(f(\omega^i)=g(\omega^i)\,h(\omega^i)\), hence \(f(\omega^i)/g(\omega^i)=h(\omega^i)\). So step 2 produces exactly the \(n\) values of \(h\) at the roots of unity. Intuition. Polynomial division is painful with coefficients but effortless at sample points, and the FFT lets us hop to and from those sample points cheaply.
