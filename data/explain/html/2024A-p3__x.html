Take \(p(n)=10n^5\) and give every edge an independent uniform weight from \(\{1,\dots,p(n)\}\). We bound the failure chance for one pair and then union-bound over all pairs.

Fix vertices \(u,v\) and let \(\mathcal{F}_{u,v}\) be the family of all \(u\)-\(v\) paths, each viewed as its set of edges. The number of edges is \(m\le\binom{n}{2}\le n^2\). By the isolation lemma, when \(m\) elements get independent uniform weights from a range of size \(p(n)\), the minimum-weight member of \(\mathcal{F}_{u,v}\) is unique except with probability at most

\[ \frac{m}{p(n)}\ \le\ \frac{n^2}{10n^5}\ =\ \frac{1}{10n^3}. \]

Call the pair \(u,v\) bad if its lightest path is not unique; then each pair is bad with probability at most \(1/(10n^3)\). There are \(\binom{n}{2}\le n^2\) pairs, so by the union bound the probability that some pair is bad is at most

\[ \binom{n}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{10n^3}\ \le\ \frac{1}{10n}\ \le\ \frac{1}{10}. \]

Hence with probability at least \(1-\tfrac{1}{10}=0.9\) no pair is bad, so every pair \(u,v\) has a unique minimum-weight path, as required.

Intuition. One random weighting quietly breaks all the ties at once, and the isolation bound depends only on the number of edges, not on how many paths there are.
