Claim False — no nonzero 5-variable polynomial vanishes at every integer point.

Strategy Use Schwartz–Zippel on a small integer grid to produce a point where the polynomial is nonzero.

Key idea A nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial has very few roots on a grid, so a non-root must exist among finitely many integer points.

Why this move "Does a nonzero polynomial have a non-root?" is precisely what Schwartz–Zippel answers, so it is the natural tool.

  1. Set up. Let \(f(x_1,\dots,x_5)\) be nonzero of total degree \(d\); we want an integer point where it doesn't vanish.
  2. Choose a grid. Take \(S=\{1,2,\dots,d+1\}\), so \(|S|=d+1\), and consider assignments in \(S^5\).
  3. Apply Schwartz–Zippel. For a uniform point of \(S^5\), \(\Pr[f=0]\le \dfrac{d}{|S|}=\dfrac{d}{d+1}<1\).
  4. Conclude. Since the chance of hitting a root is below \(1\), some \((\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_5)\in S^5\) has \(f(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_5)\neq 0\).

Those \(\alpha\)'s are integers, so we found an integer assignment where \(f\) is nonzero — a nonzero polynomial cannot vanish everywhere, hence the statement is false.
