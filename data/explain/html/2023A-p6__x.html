Associate with \(A\) the polynomial \(f_A(x)=\sum_{i\in A}x^i\) and with \(B\) the polynomial \(f_B(x)=\sum_{j\in B}x^j\); the coefficient of \(x^i\) is \(1\) exactly when \(i\) is in the set, and \(0\) otherwise. Consider the product \(g=f_A\cdot f_B\). Writing \(a_i,b_j\) for the coefficients of \(f_A,f_B\), the coefficient of \(x^k\) in \(g\) is

\[\sum_{i+j=k} a_i\,b_j.\]

Each term \(a_i b_j\) is \(0\) or \(1\), and it is \(1\) if and only if \(i\in A\) and \(j\in B\). So the coefficient of \(x^k\) in \(g\) counts exactly the number of ways to write \(k=i+j\) with \(i\in A\) and \(j\in B\). Therefore \(k\in A+B\) precisely when this coefficient is positive, and its value is the number of representations of \(k\) as such a sum.

The algorithm computes \(g=f_A\cdot f_B\) and reads off its coefficients: the positive-coefficient exponents give \(A+B\), and each coefficient gives the corresponding count.

Runtime: both \(f_A,f_B\) have degree at most \(n\), so their product can be computed in \(O(n\log n)\) using the FFT-based multiplication seen in class; building the polynomials and scanning the coefficients is \(O(n)\), for \(O(n\log n)\) total.

Intuition. Multiplying the two indicator polynomials adds every exponent of one to every exponent of the other, so it counts sums for free.
