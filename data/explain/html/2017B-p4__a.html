We must show that for any set of \(m\) three-literal clauses there is an assignment satisfying at least \(m/2\) of them. The proof pairs each assignment with its bitwise complement.

  1. The pairing. Fix any assignment \(x\), and let \(\bar x\) be its negation: \(\bar x\) assigns to each variable the opposite value it has in \(x\). We compare how many clauses \(x\) satisfies with how many \(\bar x\) satisfies.
  2. Every clause is caught by one of them. Take any clause, a disjunction of three literals. If \(x\) does not satisfy it, then all three of its literals are false under \(x\). Passing to \(\bar x\) flips every variable, hence flips every literal to true, so \(\bar x\) satisfies that clause. Therefore each clause is satisfied by \(x\), by \(\bar x\), or by both — never by neither.
  3. Count. Summing over all \(m\) clauses, \[ (\text{clauses satisfied by } x) + (\text{clauses satisfied by } \bar x) \ge m, \] because each clause contributes at least \(1\) to the left side.
If two nonnegative numbers sum to at least \(m\), the larger is at least \(m/2\). So at least one of \(x,\bar x\) satisfies at least \(m/2\) clauses, proving that such an assignment always exists.
