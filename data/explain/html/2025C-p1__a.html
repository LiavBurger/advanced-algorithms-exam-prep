The statement is true. Here "root of unity of order \(k\)" means any complex number \(\omega\) with \(\omega^k=1\) (a \(k\)-th root of unity, not necessarily primitive). We are told \(k\mid n\) and must show every such \(\omega\) also satisfies \(\omega^n=1\).

  1. Since \(k\) divides \(n\), we can write \(n=km\) for some natural number \(m\).
  2. Let \(\omega\) be any root of unity of order \(k\), so by definition \(\omega^k=1\).
  3. Then \(\omega^n=\omega^{km}=(\omega^k)^m=1^m=1\), using the exponent law \(\omega^{km}=(\omega^k)^m\).
  4. Thus \(\omega^n=1\), i.e. \(\omega\) is a root of unity of order \(n\). As \(\omega\) was arbitrary, every order-\(k\) root of unity is an order-\(n\) root of unity.
Geometrically, the \(k\)-th roots of unity are the \(k\) evenly spaced points on the unit circle, and when \(k\mid n\) they form a subset of the \(n\) evenly spaced \(n\)-th roots of unity.
