Consider any assignment \(x\) together with its bitwise negation \(\bar x\), which assigns to each variable the opposite of its value in \(x\). We claim every clause is satisfied by at least one of \(x,\bar x\). Indeed, if a clause is not satisfied by \(x\), then all three of its literals are false under \(x\); flipping every variable turns all three literals true, so the clause is satisfied by \(\bar x\). Hence no clause is failed by both \(x\) and \(\bar x\).

Summing over all \(m\) clauses, the number satisfied by \(x\) plus the number satisfied by \(\bar x\) is at least \(m\), since each clause contributes at least \(1\) to this sum. Two nonnegative numbers adding to at least \(m\) cannot both be below \(m/2\), so at least one of \(x,\bar x\) satisfies at least \(m/2\) clauses. Therefore an assignment satisfying at least \(m/2\) clauses always exists.

Intuition. An assignment and its exact opposite together cover every clause, so one of the two must handle at least half of them.
