Claim True — when every vertex has degree at most \(\Delta\), the min-degree greedy runs in polynomial time and is a \((\Delta+1)\)-approximation for maximum independent set.

Strategy Show the output is a valid independent set, then bound how many vertices each greedy pick can destroy, and compare the set's size to the trivial upper bound on the optimum.

Key idea Every iteration adds one vertex to the answer but deletes at most \(\Delta+1\) vertices from the graph, so the answer's size is at least a \(1/(\Delta+1)\) fraction of all vertices.

Why this move With no exact formula for the optimum, the standard approximation move is to sandwich it: lower-bound what greedy gets and upper-bound what anyone could get, both against the same simple quantity \(|V|\).

  1. Output is independent. Whenever a vertex \(v\) is added to \(S\), all of its neighbors are removed from \(G\), so no later pick can be adjacent to \(v\). Hence \(S\) has no internal edges.
  2. Trivial ceiling on the optimum. An independent set is just a subset of vertices, so \(\mathrm{OPT}\le |V|\).
  3. Cost of each pick. Let \(k\) be the number of iterations. Each iteration adds exactly one vertex to \(S\) and removes \(v\) together with its neighbors — at most \(1+\Delta\) vertices. So \(|S|=k\) and the total removed is at most \(k(\Delta+1)\).
  4. Counting bound. Greedy stops only when \(G\) is empty, i.e. all \(|V|\) vertices are gone, so \(k(\Delta+1)\ge |V|\), giving \(k\ge \frac{|V|}{\Delta+1}\ge \frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{\Delta+1}\).
  5. Running time. There are at most \(|V|\) iterations; keeping vertex degrees in a heap lets us find the minimum-degree vertex and update after deletions in polynomial time.

So greedy always lands within a factor \(\Delta+1\) of the best possible. The intuition is simple: each vertex you take can only knock out a handful of others (its \(\le\Delta\) neighbors plus itself), so you can never fall too far behind the optimum.
