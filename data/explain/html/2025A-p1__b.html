Claim True — the coefficients of \(f(x)^n\) can be computed in \(O(n^2 \log n)\) arithmetic operations.

Strategy Reach \(f^n\) by repeated squaring (\(f^2, f^4, \dots, f^n\)), doing each polynomial multiplication with FFT, and sum the costs.

Key idea Repeated squaring needs only \(\log n\) multiplications, and each FFT multiply of degree-\(D\) polynomials costs \(O(D \log D)\).

Why this move Raising to a power with a target op-count is the signal for fast exponentiation, and multiplying polynomials cheaply is exactly what FFT is for.

  1. Squaring ladder. Since \(n\) is a power of \(2\), compute \(f^2, f^4, f^8, \dots, f^n\) by squaring the previous result — just \(\log n\) multiplications, not \(n\).
  2. Cost of step \(i\). The \(i\)-th squaring works on polynomials of degree about \(n\cdot 2^i\), costing \(O(n\,2^i \log(n\,2^i))\) via FFT.
  3. Sum it up. Since \(2^i \le n\), each term is \(O(n\,2^i \log n)\), and \(\sum_{i=1}^{\log n} O(n\,2^i \log n) = O(n\log n)\cdot\sum 2^i = O(n^2 \log n)\).

So the whole computation stays within \(O(n^2 \log n)\) operations, dominated by the final squaring — matching the claim.
