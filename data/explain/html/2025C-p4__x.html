The two programs are equal iff the polynomials they compute are equal, so we test whether the difference is identically zero using a random evaluation. First bound the degree: each entry of a matrix has degree at most \(1\), and each matrix multiplication adds at most \(1\) to the degree, so after \(m\) products the \((1,1)\)-entries \(P_A\) of \(A_1\cdots A_m\) and \(P_B\) of \(B_1\cdots B_m\), and their difference \(Q=P_A-P_B\), all have degree at most \(m\).

The algorithm:

  1. Assign each variable \(x_i\) an independent, uniformly random value from the set \(\{1,2,\dots,100m\}\).
  2. Compute both matrix products \(A_1\cdots A_m\) and \(B_1\cdots B_m\) numerically at this assignment, and read off their \((1,1)\)-entries.
  3. Answer "equal" if the two entries are equal, and "different" otherwise.

Correctness: if the programs compute the same polynomial then \(Q\equiv 0\), the two entries always agree, and the test never errs. If they compute different polynomials then \(Q\neq 0\) has degree at most \(m\), so by the Schwartz-Zippel lemma the chance that our random point is a root is at most \(\dfrac{m}{100m}=\dfrac{1}{100}<0.1\). Thus the error probability is below \(0.1\). Runtime: each run is \(m\) multiplications of \(3\times 3\) matrices, which is efficient.

Intuition. A nonzero low-degree polynomial almost never vanishes at a random point, so plugging in random values and comparing catches any difference with high probability.
