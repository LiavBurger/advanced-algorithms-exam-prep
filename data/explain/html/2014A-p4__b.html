Take \(G\) to be the \(4\)-cycle \(C_4\) on vertices \(a,b,c,d\) (edges \(ab,bc,cd,da\)), with all weights equal to \(1\).

Integer optimum. A single vertex dominates only itself and its two neighbors — three of the four vertices — and misses the opposite vertex, so no one vertex dominates all of \(C_4\). Two vertices do suffice (for example the opposite pair \(a,c\): \(a\) dominates \(a,b,d\) and \(c\) dominates \(b,c,d\)), so the minimum dominating set has cost \(2\).

Fractional solution. Set \(x_a=0\) and \(x_b=x_c=x_d=\tfrac12\). Each vertex's closed neighborhood sums to at least \(1\): for \(a\), \(x_a+x_b+x_d=0+\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1\); for \(b\), \(x_a+x_b+x_c=0+\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1\); for \(c\), \(x_b+x_c+x_d=\tfrac32\); for \(d\), \(x_c+x_d+x_a=\tfrac12+\tfrac12+0=1\). So this point is LP-feasible, with cost

\[0+\tfrac12+\tfrac12+\tfrac12=\tfrac32.\]

Thus the LP optimum is at most \(\tfrac32\), while every integer solution costs at least \(2\). Since \(\tfrac32<2\), the LP optimum lies strictly below the ILP optimum and so cannot be attained at an integral point.

Intuition. On a square, integer domination forces two whole vertices, but spreading half-weights around covers everyone for less.
