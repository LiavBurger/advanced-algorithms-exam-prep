Claim two \(n\)-bit integers can be multiplied in \(O(n^{\log_2 3})\) time (Karatsuba).

Strategy split each number into high/low halves, expand the product, and compute it with only three half-size multiplications instead of four.

Key idea the middle term \(bc+ad\) is recovered from the single product \((a+b)(c+d)\) by subtracting off \(ac\) and \(bd\), which we compute anyway.

Why this move the naive expansion needs four half-products, giving \(T(n)=4T(n/2)+O(n)=O(n^2)\); killing one multiplication drops the branching factor to \(3\) and beats \(n^2\).

  1. Split. Write \(A=a\,2^{n/2}+b\) and \(C=c\,2^{n/2}+d\), where \(a,b,c,d\) are \(n/2\)-bit numbers (high and low halves; the \(2^{n/2}\) is a shift).
  2. Expand. \(A\cdot C=ac\,2^n+(bc+ad)\,2^{n/2}+bd\). Naively this is the four products \(ac,bc,ad,bd\).
  3. The trick. Since \((a+b)(c+d)=ac+ad+bc+bd\), we get \(bc+ad=(a+b)(c+d)-ac-bd\). So we only need three half-size products: \(ac,\ bd,\ (a+b)(c+d)\).
  4. Cost of the glue. The remaining additions and shifts (by \(2^n,2^{n/2}\)) are each \(O(n)\) by the allowed basic operations, so the non-recursive work is \(O(n)\): \(T(n)=3T(n/2)+O(n)\).
  5. Solve. Master Theorem with \(a=3,b=2,k=1\): since \(3>2^1\), \(T(n)=\Theta\!\big(n^{\log_2 3}\big)\approx O(n^{1.585})\). Intuitively, saving one of every four multiplications compounds across all the recursion levels into a genuinely smaller exponent.
