The statement is true, and it follows from the spectral mixing analysis of random walks on regular graphs seen in class. Let \(A\) be the normalized adjacency matrix of \(G\); because \(G\) is regular, \(A\) is exactly the transition matrix of the random walk, its largest eigenvalue is \(1\) with the uniform vector \(\tfrac1n\mathbf{1}\) as eigenvector, and by assumption every other eigenvalue has absolute value at most \(1/2\).

  1. Track the distribution. Start at \(s\); the initial distribution \(v\) is the indicator vector on \(s\). After \(k\) steps the distribution is \(A^k v\).
  2. Decompose in the eigenbasis. Write \(v\) along the uniform direction plus a remainder orthogonal to \(\mathbf 1\). Applying \(A^k\) leaves the uniform component fixed (eigenvalue \(1\)) and shrinks every other component by its eigenvalue to the \(k\)-th power, which is at most \((1/2)^k\) in magnitude.
  3. Choose \(k=O(\log n)\). Then \((1/2)^k\) is polynomially small, and one gets \(A^k v = \tfrac1n\mathbf 1 + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\) where the error vector \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\) has norm at most \(n^{-100}\).
The probability of being at \(t\) after \(k\) steps is the \(t\)-th entry of \(A^k v\), namely \(\tfrac1n + \varepsilon_t\). Since \(|\varepsilon_t|\le \|\boldsymbol\varepsilon\|\le n^{-100}\), this entry is at least \(\tfrac1n - n^{-100} \ge \tfrac{1}{2n}\). So for a suitable constant \(C\) the walk reaches \(t\) within \(C\log n\) steps with probability at least \(\tfrac{1}{2n}\).
