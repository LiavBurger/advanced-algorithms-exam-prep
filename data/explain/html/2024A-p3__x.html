We must find a polynomial \(p(n)\) so that random edge weights from \(\{1,\dots,p(n)\}\) make the minimum-weight path unique for every pair \(u,v\) simultaneously, with probability \(\ge0.9\). The plan is: apply the Isolation Lemma to a single fixed pair to bound the chance its optimum is not unique, then take a union bound over all pairs.

  1. Recall the Isolation Lemma. Given a universe of \(m\) elements, each assigned an independent uniformly random integer weight in \(\{1,\dots,N\}\), and any family of subsets of the universe, the probability that the minimum-weight subset in the family is not unique is at most \(m/N\).
  2. Set up one pair. Fix \(u,v\) and let the family \(\mathcal F_{u,v}\) be all \(u\)–\(v\) paths, each identified with its set of edges; the universe is the edge set \(E\). The number of edges is \(m\le\binom{n}{2}\le n^2\). Choose \(p(n)=10n^5\) as the weight range \(N\).
  3. Bound a "bad" pair. Call \(u,v\) bad if the minimum-weight \(u\)–\(v\) path is not unique. By the lemma the probability of this is at most \[ \frac{m}{p(n)}\le \frac{n^2}{10n^5}=\frac{1}{10n^3}. \]
  4. Union bound over all pairs. There are \(\binom{n}{2}\le n^2\) pairs. The probability that some pair is bad is at most \[ \binom{n}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{10n^3}\le n^2\cdot\frac{1}{10n^3}=\frac{1}{10n}\le\frac{1}{10}. \]
With \(p(n)=10n^5\), the probability that any pair fails is at most \(1/10\); hence with probability at least \(0.9\) every pair \(u,v\) has a unique minimum-weight path.

The same random weights are used for all pairs at once, which is why the single union bound over the \(\binom n2\) pairs suffices.
