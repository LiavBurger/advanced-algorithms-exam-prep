Claim \(\mathrm{MATCH}\le\mathrm{OPT}/2\), where \(\mathrm{MATCH}\) is the min-weight perfect matching and \(\mathrm{OPT}\) the min TSP tour.

Strategy split an optimal tour's edges into two perfect matchings; the cheaper one is at most half the tour, and \(\mathrm{MATCH}\) is no larger.

Key idea a cycle on an even number of vertices decomposes into two disjoint perfect matchings by taking alternate edges.

Why this move to upper-bound the best matching, the natural trick is to exhibit a cheap matching, and a tour hands you two of them for free.

  1. Setup. Assume \(n\) even (needed for a perfect matching). Let \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) be the vertices in the order of a minimum TSP tour, whose \(n\) edges \(x_1x_2,\dots,x_nx_1\) total \(\mathrm{OPT}\).
  2. Two matchings from the tour. Take alternate edges: \(M_1=\{x_1x_2,x_3x_4,\dots,x_{n-1}x_n\}\) and \(M_2=\{x_2x_3,x_4x_5,\dots,x_nx_1\}\). Each touches every vertex once, so both are perfect matchings, and together they use every tour edge exactly once: \(w(M_1)+w(M_2)=\mathrm{OPT}\).
  3. One is cheap. Since the two weights sum to \(\mathrm{OPT}\), the smaller satisfies \(\min\{w(M_1),w(M_2)\}\le\mathrm{OPT}/2\) (the min is at most the average).
  4. Conclude. \(\mathrm{MATCH}\) is the minimum over all perfect matchings, so \(\mathrm{MATCH}\le\min\{w(M_1),w(M_2)\}\le\mathrm{OPT}/2\).
