False. By the strong duality theorem, if a standard-form LP \((P)\) and its dual \((D)\) are both feasible, then both have finite optima and those optimal values are equal. So the optimum of \((P)\) can never be strictly greater than the optimum of \((D)\).

To be concrete, take \((P)=\min c^\top x\) s.t. \(Ax=b,\ x\ge 0\), with dual \((D)=\max b^\top y\) s.t. \(A^\top y\le c\). Weak duality always gives \(\operatorname{opt}(P)\ge\operatorname{opt}(D)\). Strong duality upgrades this: once both are feasible, the duality gap is zero and \(\operatorname{opt}(P)=\operatorname{opt}(D)\). Equal values leave no room for a strict inequality, so the statement is false.

Intuition. Once both sides are feasible, the primal and dual meet at exactly the same value, so neither can beat the other.
