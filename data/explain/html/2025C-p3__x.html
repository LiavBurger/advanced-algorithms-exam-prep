Let \(A\) be the normalized adjacency matrix of \(G\). As shown in class, since \(G\) is regular and connected, \(A\) is symmetric with eigenvalues \(\mu_n\le\cdots\le\mu_1\) satisfying \(-1\le\mu_i\le 1\), with \(\mu_1=1\) (the uniform vector is its eigenvector) and a spectral gap \(\mu_2\le 1-\tfrac1{4n^5}\).

The lazy walk is given by the matrix \(C=\tfrac13 I+\tfrac23 A\). It has the same eigenvectors as \(A\), with eigenvalues \(\lambda_i=\tfrac13+\tfrac23\mu_i\). So \(\lambda_1=\tfrac13+\tfrac23=1\), and

\[\lambda_2=\tfrac13+\tfrac23\mu_2\le \tfrac13+\tfrac23\big(1-\tfrac1{4n^5}\big)=1-\tfrac1{6n^5}.\]

Also \(\mu_n\ge -1\) gives \(\lambda_n\ge \tfrac13-\tfrac23=-\tfrac13\), so every eigenvalue lies in \([-\tfrac13,1]\); hence \(|\lambda_i|\le|\lambda_2|\le 1-\tfrac1{6n^5}\) for all \(i\ge 2\). The laziness is exactly what keeps the smallest eigenvalue away from \(-1\).

Now run the standard mixing argument. Let \(e_s\) be the indicator vector of the start vertex \(s\); after \(T\) steps the distribution is \(p_T=C^T e_s\). Since \(C\) is symmetric, its unit eigenvectors \(v_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt n}\mathbf 1,v_2,\dots,v_n\) form an orthonormal basis. Writing \(e_s=\sum_i\langle e_s,v_i\rangle v_i\),

\[p_T=C^T e_s=\sum_i \lambda_i^{T}\langle e_s,v_i\rangle v_i.\]

The \(i=1\) term is \(\langle e_s,v_1\rangle v_1=\tfrac1n\mathbf 1=\pi\), the uniform distribution. Subtracting it and using orthonormality,

\[\|p_T-\pi\|_2^2=\sum_{i\ge 2}\lambda_i^{2T}\langle e_s,v_i\rangle^2\le |\lambda_2|^{2T}\sum_{i\ge 2}\langle e_s,v_i\rangle^2\le|\lambda_2|^{2T}\,\|e_s\|_2^2=|\lambda_2|^{2T},\]

so \(\|p_T-\pi\|_2\le|\lambda_2|^{T}\le\big(1-\tfrac1{6n^5}\big)^{T}\).

Pick \(T=6n^5\ln(2n)\), which is polynomial in \(n\). Using \((1-x)^T\le e^{-xT}\), the error shrinks fast:

\[\|p_T-\pi\|_2\le e^{-T/(6n^5)}=e^{-\ln(2n)}=\tfrac1{2n}.\]

Finally, the single coordinate at the target \(t\) is within the \(\ell_2\) distance of the uniform value:

\[\big|p_T(t)-\tfrac1n\big|\le\|p_T-\pi\|_2\le\tfrac1{2n},\]

so \(p_T(t)\ge \tfrac1n-\tfrac1{2n}=\tfrac1{2n}\). Thus after \(T=\mathrm{poly}(n)\) steps the walk is at \(t\) with probability at least \(\tfrac1{2n}\).

Intuition. Laziness stops the walk from oscillating, so it spreads out to nearly uniform, and uniform already puts about \(1/n\) on \(t\).
