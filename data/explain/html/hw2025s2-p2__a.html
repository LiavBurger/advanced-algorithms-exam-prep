Let \(A\) be the normalized adjacency matrix of the \(d\)-regular graph \(G\) on \(n\) vertices (so \(A_{u,v}=1/d\) if \((u,v)\in E\) and \(0\) otherwise), with eigenvalues \(1=\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n\). The all-ones vector \(\mathbf 1\) is always an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(1\). We exhibit a second eigenvector for eigenvalue \(1\) that is independent of \(\mathbf 1\), which forces the multiplicity of \(1\) to be at least \(2\), hence \(\lambda_2=1\).

  1. Pick a component and its indicator. Since \(G\) is disconnected, choose a connected component \(S\) with \(\emptyset\neq S\subsetneq V\). Let \(x\) be the indicator vector of \(S\): \(x_v=1\) if \(v\in S\) and \(x_v=0\) otherwise.
  2. \(x\) is independent of \(\mathbf 1\). Because \(S\) is neither empty nor all of \(V\), the vector \(x\) has both \(1\)-entries and \(0\)-entries, so it is not a scalar multiple of \(\mathbf 1\).
  3. \(x\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(1\), i.e. \(Ax=x\). For any vertex \(v\), \((Ax)_v=\sum_{u:(u,v)\in E}\frac1d x_u\). If \(v\in S\), then since \(S\) is a component every neighbour \(u\) of \(v\) also lies in \(S\), so \(x_u=1\); by \(d\)-regularity there are exactly \(d\) such neighbours, giving \((Ax)_v=d\cdot\frac1d=1=x_v\). If \(v\notin S\), then no neighbour of \(v\) lies in \(S\), so the sum is \(0=x_v\). Hence \(Ax=x\).
  4. Conclude. Eigenvalue \(1\) now has two independent eigenvectors, \(\mathbf 1\) and \(x\), so its multiplicity is at least \(2\); as \(1\) is the largest eigenvalue, \(\lambda_2=1\). The same argument with \(k\) components (using \(k\) independent component-indicators) shows \(\lambda_1=\lambda_2=\cdots=\lambda_k=1\).
