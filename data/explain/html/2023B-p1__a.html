The statement is true, and it is exactly the content of the strong duality theorem for linear programming. Recall the setup: to every primal LP (say a minimization \(\min c^\top x\) over a polyhedron \(\{x : Ax \ge b,\ x \ge 0\}\)) there is an associated dual LP (a maximization). We must argue that when the primal polyhedron is bounded and non-empty, the two optimal objective values coincide.

  1. The primal has a finite optimum. "Non-empty" means the primal is feasible (there is at least one \(x\) in the polyhedron). "Bounded" means the objective cannot run off to \(\pm\infty\) over the feasible region. A feasible LP whose objective is bounded attains its optimum at a vertex, so the primal optimal value is a finite number.
  2. Weak duality gives one inequality. For every primal-feasible \(x\) and dual-feasible \(y\), weak duality gives \(\text{(dual objective)} \le \text{(primal objective)}\). So the dual is bounded above by the finite primal optimum and cannot be unbounded.
  3. Strong duality closes the gap. The duality theorem states that if the primal is feasible and bounded, then the dual is also feasible, and the two optimal objective values are equal, not merely ordered by weak duality.
The parenthetical remark is the contrapositive intuition: if the dual were infeasible, the primal would be unbounded (or infeasible). Since here the primal is feasible and bounded, the dual must be feasible, and both optima agree.
