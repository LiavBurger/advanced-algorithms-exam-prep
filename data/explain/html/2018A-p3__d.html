We add an auxiliary variable \(z\) that bounds the absolute value from above by two linear inequalities, and minimize \(z\): \[\min z \quad\text{s.t.}\quad 4x-5\ge 0,\qquad z\ge 2x-10,\qquad z\ge -(2x-10).\]

The idea is that for any number \(t\), the value \(|t|\) is the smallest \(z\) satisfying both \(z\ge t\) and \(z\ge -t\). Taking \(t=2x-10\), the constraints \(z\ge 2x-10\) and \(z\ge -2x+10\) together say \(z\ge |2x-10|\).

Minimizing \(z\) then drives it down until one of the two inequalities is tight, so at the optimum \(z=|2x-10|\). No separate sign constraint on \(z\) is needed, since the two inequalities already force \(z\ge 0\).

The original constraint \(4x-5\ge 0\) is already linear, so it stays unchanged. Everything is now linear in \(x\) and \(z\), and the optimal \(z\) equals \(\min|2x-10|\) subject to \(4x-5\ge 0\).

Intuition. An absolute value in a minimization becomes a new variable squeezed above both branches, \(z\ge t\) and \(z\ge -t\).
