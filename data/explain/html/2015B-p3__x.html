We prove \(\mathrm{MATCH}\le\mathrm{OPT}/2\). The idea is to take an optimal tour, split its edges into two matchings by taking alternate edges, and observe that the cheaper of the two is a matching of weight at most \(\mathrm{OPT}/2\), while \(\mathrm{MATCH}\) is no larger than any matching.

Assume without loss of generality that \(n\) is even (otherwise a maximum matching cannot cover every vertex, and the two matchings below are still maximum matchings by the same argument). Let \(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n\) be the vertices of a minimum weight traveling salesperson tour, listed in the order they are visited. The tour uses the \(n\) edges

\[ x_1x_2,\; x_2x_3,\; \dots,\; x_{n-1}x_n,\; x_nx_1, \]

and their total weight is \(\mathrm{OPT}\).

Split these edges into two sets by taking alternate edges of the cycle:

\[ M_1=\{x_1x_2,\;x_3x_4,\;\dots,\;x_{n-1}x_n\},\qquad M_2=\{x_2x_3,\;x_4x_5,\;\dots,\;x_nx_1\}. \]

Each vertex is an endpoint of exactly one edge in \(M_1\) and exactly one edge in \(M_2\), so both \(M_1\) and \(M_2\) are (maximum) matchings in \(G\). Together they use each of the \(n\) tour edges exactly once, so

\[ w(M_1)+w(M_2)=\mathrm{OPT}. \]

Since the two weights sum to \(\mathrm{OPT}\), the smaller of them is at most the average:

\[ \min\{w(M_1),\,w(M_2)\}\le\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{2}. \]

Finally, \(\mathrm{MATCH}\) is the minimum weight over all maximum matchings of \(G\), so it is no larger than either \(M_1\) or \(M_2\). Combining the two facts,

\[ \mathrm{MATCH}\le\min\{w(M_1),\,w(M_2)\}\le\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{2}, \]

which is exactly what we wanted to prove.

Intuition. A tour is a cycle, and a cycle falls apart into two matchings by taking every other edge, so one of them must cost at most half the tour.
