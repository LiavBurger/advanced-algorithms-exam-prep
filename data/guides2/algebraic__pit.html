When do I reach for this?

You have a polynomial that is huge to write out (a determinant, a product of many factors, a formula) and you only need to know one thing: is it the zero polynomial or not? You cannot afford to expand it — but you can plug in numbers. That is exactly Polynomial Identity Testing (PIT): test \(p\equiv 0\) by evaluating \(p\) at a random point.

  • "Are two expressions/circuits/matrices equal?" → test their difference \(p=A-B\) is the zero polynomial.
  • "Does this graph have a perfect matching / a subspace contain an invertible matrix?" → a determinant is a nonzero polynomial exactly when the object exists.
  • The polynomial has low degree \(d\) but exponentially many monomials → evaluate, don't expand.

Core tools

SCHWARTZ–ZIPPEL
Let \(p\neq 0\) be a polynomial in any number of variables of total degree \(\le d\), over a field, and let \(S\) be a finite set. For \(r_1,\dots,r_n\) chosen independently and uniformly from \(S\), \[ \Pr\big[\,p(r_1,\dots,r_n)=0\,\big]\le \frac{d}{|S|}. \] a nonzero low-degree polynomial rarely vanishes at a random point
PIT ALGORITHM
To decide \(p\equiv 0\): pick \(S\) with \(|S|\ge 2d\), draw a random point \(r\), evaluate \(p(r)\). Output "\(p\equiv0\)" iff \(p(r)=0\). One-sided error: if \(p\equiv0\) always correct; if \(p\neq0\) wrong with prob. \(\le d/|S|\le \tfrac12\). Repeat \(k\) times → error \(\le 2^{-k}\). the polynomial is expensive to expand but cheap to evaluate at a point
Theorem Univariate special case. A nonzero single-variable polynomial of degree \(d\) has at most \(d\) roots. So over any set of \(d+1\) distinct points it is nonzero at one of them. Schwartz–Zippel is the multivariate generalization (proved by induction on the number of variables — this is a homework exercise).

Worked example — the thinking

2024A P6. Given matrices \(B_1,\dots,B_k\), decide whether their span contains an invertible matrix.

  1. Build one polynomial that "sees" the whole span. A generic element of the span is \(M(x)=\sum_{i=1}^k x_i B_i\). Its determinant \(p(x_1,\dots,x_k)=\det\!\big(\sum_i x_i B_i\big)\) is a polynomial in the \(x_i\).
  2. Translate the question into "is \(p\) the zero polynomial?" The span contains an invertible matrix \(\iff\) some assignment makes \(\det\neq0\) \(\iff\) \(p\not\equiv0\).
  3. Bound the degree. \(\det\) of an \(n\times n\) matrix is a sum over permutations of products of \(n\) entries, each entry linear in the \(x_i\); so \(\deg p\le n\).
  4. Run PIT. Take \(S=\{1,\dots,2n\}\), plug in random integers, compute \(\det(\sum r_i B_i)\) by Gaussian elimination in \(O(n^3)\). If nonzero, answer YES; error \(\le n/|S|\le\tfrac12\), amplify by repetition.
Traps. Schwartz–Zippel bounds probability by \(d/|S|\), so you must choose \(|S|\ge 2d\) (or larger) — a small \(S\) can make the bound useless or \(>1\). The error is one-sided: "\(p(r)=0\)" is only evidence for \(p\equiv0\), never a proof. \(d\) is the total degree (sum of exponents), not the number of variables. And note: a nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial can still vanish on a huge set (e.g. all order-\(m\) roots of unity if \(d\ge m\)) — SZ only helps when \(|S|>d\).

Checklist

  1. Identify the polynomial \(p\) whose (non)vanishing answers the question — usually a determinant or a difference \(A-B\).
  2. Bound its total degree \(d\) (for a determinant, \(d\le n\)).
  3. Choose \(S\) with \(|S|\ge 2d\); sample a random point.
  4. Evaluate \(p\) at the point efficiently (never expand it).
  5. State the one-sided error \(d/|S|\) and amplify by repetition to \(2^{-k}\).
