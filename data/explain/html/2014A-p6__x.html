Split each \(n\)-bit number into a high and low half of \(n/2\) bits: write \(A=a\,2^{n/2}+b\) and \(C=c\,2^{n/2}+d\). Then

\[A\cdot C=ac\,2^{n}+(ad+bc)\,2^{n/2}+bd.\]

The middle coefficient can be obtained from just one extra product using the identity

\[ad+bc=(a+b)(c+d)-ac-bd,\]

so

\[A\cdot C=ac\,2^{n}+\big((a+b)(c+d)-ac-bd\big)2^{n/2}+bd.\]

Hence to multiply two \(n\)-bit numbers we only need three products of (about) \(n/2\)-bit numbers — \(ac\), \(bd\), and \((a+b)(c+d)\) — together with a constant number of additions and shifts of \(O(n)\)-bit numbers, each costing \(O(n)\) by the allowed basic operations. Computing the three half-size products recursively, the running time satisfies

\[T(n)=3\,T(n/2)+O(n).\]

By the Master theorem with \(a=3\), \(b=2\), and \(O(n)=O(n^k)\) with \(k=1\): since \(a=3>2=b^k\), i.e. \(\log_2 3>1\), the recursion dominates and \(T(n)=O\!\big(n^{\log_2 3}\big)\).

Intuition. The schoolbook method needs four half-products; one clever combination replaces the two middle ones, cutting four recursive calls down to three.
