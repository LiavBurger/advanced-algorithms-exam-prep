We turn part (a) into a recurrence for the running time \(T(n)\) of multiplying two \(n\)-bit integers and solve it.

  1. Set up the recurrence. Splitting \(x,y\) into halves and using part (a), the product \(xy=x_1y_1\,2^n+(x_0y_1+x_1y_0)\,2^{n/2}+x_0y_0\) is computed from just three multiplications of numbers of about \(n/2\) bits: \(x_0y_0\), \(x_1y_1\), and \((x_0+x_1)(y_0+y_1)\). Everything else is cheap: multiplying by \(2^{n}\) or \(2^{n/2}\) is a bit shift, and the additions/subtractions (including forming \(x_0+x_1\) and \(y_0+y_1\)) cost \(O(n)\). The sums \(x_0+x_1,\,y_0+y_1\) may need \(n/2+1\) bits, but that extra bit is negligible and does not change the asymptotics. Hence \[ T(n)=3\,T(n/2)+O(n). \]
  2. Solve it. This has the Master-theorem shape with \(a=3\) branches, shrink factor \(b=2\), and additive work \(O(n^{1})\). The critical exponent is \(\log_b a=\log_2 3\approx 1.585\), which exceeds the exponent \(1\) of the additive term, so the recursion is leaf-dominated.
  3. Recursion tree (why leaves win). Level \(i\) has \(3^i\) subproblems of size \(n/2^i\), contributing \(3^i\cdot O(n/2^i)=O\!\big(n(3/2)^i\big)\). Summing the geometric series over \(i=0,\dots,\log_2 n\) is dominated by the last level: \[ T(n)=O\!\Big(n\sum_{i=0}^{\log_2 n}(3/2)^i\Big)=O\!\big(n\,(3/2)^{\log_2 n}\big)=O\!\big(3^{\log_2 n}\big)=O\!\big(n^{\log_2 3}\big). \]

So the recurrence solves to \(T(n)=O(n^{\log_2 3})\), beating the \(O(n^2)\) schoolbook method.
