The plan is to show the coordinates of \(x\) sum to zero; since one of them (\(x_i\)) is at least \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}>0\), the remaining coordinates must sum to a negative number, forcing one of them to be nonpositive. The heart of the argument is establishing \(\sum_k x_k=0\), i.e. that \(x\) is orthogonal to the all-ones vector \(\mathbf 1\).

  1. \(\mathbf 1\) is the top eigenvector. Each row of the normalized adjacency matrix \(A\) sums to \(1\), so \(A\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1\); hence \(B\mathbf 1=(\tfrac12 I+\tfrac12 A)\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1\). Thus \(\mathbf 1\) is an eigenvector of \(B\) with eigenvalue \(1=\lambda_1\).
  2. \(\lambda_2<1\) strictly. Suppose \(By=y\) and pick \(i\) with \(y_i\) maximal. Row \(i\) of \(By=y\) reads \(y_i=\tfrac12 y_i+\tfrac1{2d}\sum_{j\sim i}y_j\), so \(y_i=\tfrac1d\sum_{j\sim i}y_j\), the average of its neighbors. Each \(y_j\le y_i\) yet they average to \(y_i\), forcing \(y_j=y_i\) for every neighbor. As \(G\) is connected, iterating makes \(y\) constant, i.e. a multiple of \(\mathbf 1\). So the \(1\)-eigenspace is \(\operatorname{span}(\mathbf 1)\), and therefore \(\lambda_2<1\).
  3. Orthogonality. \(B\) is real symmetric, so eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. Since \(x\) belongs to \(\lambda_2<1\) and \(\mathbf 1\) to \(1\), \(\langle x,\mathbf 1\rangle=\sum_k x_k=0\).
  4. Finding \(j\). Using \(x_i\ge\tfrac1{\sqrt n}\), \(\sum_{k\ne i}x_k=\big(\sum_k x_k\big)-x_i=-x_i\le-\tfrac1{\sqrt n}<0\). A sum that is negative cannot have all its terms positive, so some coordinate \(j\ne i\) satisfies \(x_j\le0\).
