The statement is True, and the reason is linearity of expectation.

  1. Linearity of expectation says that for any random variables \(x,y\) on the same sample space and any constants \(a,b\), \(E[ax+by]=aE[x]+bE[y]\). Applying it with \(a=2\), \(b=-1\) gives exactly \(E[2x-y]=2E[x]-E[y]\).
  2. The crucial point the problem is testing: this identity requires no independence assumption. Linearity follows directly from the definition of expectation as a sum (or integral) over the sample space, \[ E[2x-y]=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\Pr[\omega]\,\bigl(2x(\omega)-y(\omega)\bigr) =2\sum_{\omega}\Pr[\omega]x(\omega)-\sum_{\omega}\Pr[\omega]y(\omega), \] which is just \(2E[x]-E[y]\). No term ever requires the joint distribution to factor.

Independence is needed only for statements about products, such as \(E[xy]=E[x]E[y]\); it is irrelevant for linear combinations. So the equation holds for arbitrary, possibly dependent, \(x\) and \(y\).
