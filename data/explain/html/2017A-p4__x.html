Take \(n\) independent samples, output the sample mean as the estimate, and use a Chernoff bound to fix \(n\); about \(30000\ln 20\) samples suffice, independent of the stream length.

Let \(X_i\) be an indicator random variable for whether the \(i\)-th sampled bit is a one, and let \(\mu\) be the true fraction of ones. The samples are independent with \(\mathbb{E}[X_i]=\mu\), and the estimate is \(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\).

By the Chernoff bound (both tails, hence the factor \(2\)), \[\Pr\!\left[\Big|\tfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i-\mu\Big|\ge 0.01\right]\le 2\,e^{-\frac{n(0.01)^2}{3\mu}}.\] We want this to be at most \(1-0.9=0.1\). Taking logarithms, it suffices that \(\ln 20\le \frac{n(0.01)^2}{3\mu}=\frac{n}{30000\,\mu}\), i.e. \[n\ge 30000\,\mu\,\ln 20.\]

Since \(\mu\le 1\) always, taking \(n=30000\ln 20\) works for every possible \(\mu\). So \(30000\ln 20\approx 90000\) samples guarantee the estimate is within \(0.01\) of the true frequency with probability at least \(0.9\); note this is a fixed constant, independent of the sequence length.

Intuition. Chernoff sets the number of samples needed for accuracy \(0.01\) at confidence \(0.9\), and \(\mu\le 1\) lets us drop the unknown \(\mu\).
