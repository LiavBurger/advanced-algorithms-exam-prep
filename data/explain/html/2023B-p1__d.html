True. This is exactly the spectral mixing bound for random walks proved in class.

Let \(A\) be the normalized adjacency (transition) matrix of \(G\), and let \(v\) be the starting distribution, the indicator vector of \(s\). After \(k\) steps the distribution is \(A^k v\).

Because \(G\) is regular, the all-ones direction \(\tfrac1n\mathbf 1\) is the uniform stationary distribution and is fixed by \(A\) (its eigenvalue is \(1\)). Write \(v\) as its uniform component plus a part orthogonal to \(\mathbf 1\). Every eigenvalue other than the top one has absolute value at most \(1/2\), so applying \(A^k\) keeps the uniform part unchanged and shrinks the orthogonal part by a factor of at most \((1/2)^k\).

Take \(k = C\log n\) for a suitable constant \(C\). Then \((1/2)^k\) is extremely small, so \[ A^k v = \tfrac1n\mathbf 1 + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \] where the error vector \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\) has norm below \(n^{-100}\) (in particular below \(\tfrac1{2n}\)).

The probability that the walk is at \(t\) after \(k\) steps is the \(t\)-th entry of \(A^k v\), which is \(\tfrac1n + \varepsilon_t \ge \tfrac1n - n^{-100} \ge \tfrac1{2n}\). Hence for every pair \(s,t\) the walk reaches \(t\) within \(C\log n\) steps with probability at least \(\tfrac1{2n}\), so the statement holds.

Intuition. A big gap between the top eigenvalue and the rest means the walk forgets where it started in \(O(\log n)\) steps and spreads out almost uniformly.
