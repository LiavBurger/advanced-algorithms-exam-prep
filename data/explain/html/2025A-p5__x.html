Goal Give an efficient randomized algorithm deciding whether two directed graphs \(G_1,G_2\) are determinantally equivalent — their symbolic adjacency determinants are equal as polynomials — with error below \(0.1\).

Strategy Don't expand the determinants; instead test the polynomial identity \(\det(A_1)=\det(A_2)\) by plugging in random values and comparing the two numbers.

Key idea The difference \(P=\det(A_1)-\det(A_2)\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n\); a random evaluation reveals whether it is the zero polynomial.

Why this move "Are these two huge symbolic expressions equal" is polynomial identity testing, and the reflex tool is Schwartz-Zippel: evaluate at a random point over a large enough set.

  1. The algorithm. For each variable \(x_{i,j}\), pick an independent uniform integer \(r_{i,j}\in\{1,\dots,100n\}\), substitute it in, and compute both determinants numerically (each a plain number-matrix determinant, polynomial time). Declare equivalence iff the two numbers match.
  2. Bound the degree. Let \(A_i\) be the symbolic adjacency matrix of \(G_i\) and \(P=\det(A_1)-\det(A_2)\). A determinant of an \(n\times n\) matrix is a sum of products of \(n\) entries, so \(\deg(P)\le n\).
  3. Equivalent case. If the graphs are equivalent, \(P\equiv 0\), so it evaluates to \(0\) for any choice of \(r_{i,j}\); the determinants always agree and the algorithm is correct with certainty.
  4. Non-equivalent case. If not equivalent, \(P\not\equiv 0\). By Schwartz-Zippel, a nonzero degree-\(\le n\) polynomial evaluated over a set of size \(100n\) vanishes with probability at most \(n/(100n)=1/100\).
  5. Conclude. The only error is a false "equivalent", with probability at most \(1/100<0.1\), and the algorithm just computes two determinants — so it is efficient and correct enough.
