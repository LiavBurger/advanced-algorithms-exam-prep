Karatsuba's trick splits each \(n\)-bit number into a high and low half, \(x=x_1 2^{n/2}+x_0\) and \(y=y_1 2^{n/2}+y_0\), so the product is \(xy=x_1y_1\,2^{n}+(x_0y_1+x_1y_0)\,2^{n/2}+x_0y_0\). The three coefficients needed are \(x_0y_0\), \(x_1y_1\), and the cross term \(x_0y_1+x_1y_0\). The goal here is to obtain the cross term using only one multiplication beyond the two we already compute.

  1. Expand the product of the two sums: \[ (x_0+x_1)(y_0+y_1)=x_0y_0+x_0y_1+x_1y_0+x_1y_1. \]
  2. The middle two summands are exactly the cross term. Solving for it, \[ x_0y_1+x_1y_0=(x_0+x_1)(y_0+y_1)-x_0y_0-x_1y_1. \]
  3. In the recursive multiplication we already form \(x_0y_0\) (the low coefficient) and \(x_1y_1\) (the high coefficient). So the only new multiplication required to get the cross term is the single product \((x_0+x_1)(y_0+y_1)\); the two subtractions are additions, not multiplications.

Thus the naive approach of computing \(x_0y_1\) and \(x_1y_0\) separately (two multiplications) is replaced by reusing \(x_0y_0,x_1y_1\) and paying just one extra multiplication. Three multiplications total then suffice for the whole product, which is precisely what drives the improved recurrence in part (b).
