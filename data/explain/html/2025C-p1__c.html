The statement is true, and it is a direct application of the isolation lemma. Recall the lemma: for any family \(\mathcal{F}\) of subsets of a ground set of size \(m\), if each element gets an independent uniform weight from \(\{1,\dots,N\}\) and set-weight is the sum of element weights, then the probability that the minimum-weight set in \(\mathcal{F}\) is not unique is at most \(m/N\).

  1. Take the ground set to be the edge set \(E\), so \(m=|E|\), and let \(\mathcal{F}\) be the family of edge-subsets that are exactly the cuts of \(G\). The weight of a cut is the sum of weights of its crossing edges—precisely the sum form the lemma needs.
  2. Weights are drawn uniformly and independently from \(\{1,\dots,4|E|\}\), so \(N=4|E|\). The lemma gives \(\Pr[\text{minimum cut not unique}]\le m/N=|E|/(4|E|)=\tfrac14\).
  3. For the maximum cut, apply the same argument to the negated weights (maximizing a sum is minimizing its negation; the value set is still \(N\) distinct values). This yields \(\Pr[\text{maximum cut not unique}]\le \tfrac14\) as well.
  4. By a union bound, the probability that at least one of the two failure events occurs is at most \(\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12\).
  5. Therefore, with probability at least \(1-\tfrac12=\tfrac12\), both the minimum-weight cut and the maximum-weight cut are unique.
