True. This is exactly linearity of expectation, which holds for any random variables whether or not they are independent.

For any two random variables \(x,y\) on the same sample space and any constants \(a,b\), we have \(E[ax+by]=a\,E[x]+b\,E[y]\). This follows directly from the definition of expectation as a sum over outcomes weighted by their probabilities: summation is linear, so constants pull out and the expectation of a sum is the sum of the expectations. Nowhere does this use independence.

Applying it with \(a=2\) and \(b=-1\) gives

\[ E[2x-y]=2E[x]-E[y]. \]

So the statement is always true, even though \(x\) and \(y\) may be dependent. (Independence would only be needed for a product such as \(E[xy]=E[x]E[y]\), never for a linear combination.)

Intuition. Averages add up no matter how the variables are related; dependence would only matter for products or variances, not for a plain sum.
