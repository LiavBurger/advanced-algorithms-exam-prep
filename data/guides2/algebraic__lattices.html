When do I reach for this?

The question talks about a lattice \(L=\{\sum_i \lambda_i b_i:\lambda_i\in\mathbb{Z}\}\) generated by linearly independent vectors \(b_1,\dots,b_n\), and asks about short vectors, a reduced basis, or a bound involving \(\det L\). Two ideas carry almost every exam item: coefficients are integers (so a nonzero coefficient has \(|\lambda_j|\ge1\)), and the determinant is the product of the Gram–Schmidt lengths.

  • "Is \(b_1\) a shortest nonzero vector?" → expand \(\|v\|^2\) in the basis; integrality does the work.
  • "Prove \(\|b_1\|\le 2^{(n-1)/4}(\det L)^{1/n}\) for an LLL-reduced basis" → chain the Lovász condition, then multiply.
  • Gram–Schmidt vectors \(\tilde b_i\) are orthogonal but are not in general lattice vectors; use them only for lengths and determinants.

Core tools

ORTHOGONAL BASIS ⇒ SHORTEST VECTOR
If \(b_1,\dots,b_n\) are pairwise orthogonal and \(v=\sum_i\lambda_i b_i\) with \(\lambda_i\in\mathbb{Z}\), then \[ \|v\|^2=\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i^2\|b_i\|^2 . \] Pick any \(j\) with \(\lambda_j\ne0\): \(\lambda_j^2\ge1\), so \(\|v\|^2\ge\|b_j\|^2\ge\min_i\|b_i\|^2\). Worked example — the thinking

2024C P5. Let \(b_1,\dots,b_n\) be an LLL-reduced basis of \(L\). Prove \(\|b_1\|\le 2^{\frac{n-1}{4}}(\det L)^{1/n}\).

  1. Start from the reduction guarantee. \(\|\tilde b_i\|^2\ge\tfrac12\|\tilde b_{i-1}\|^2\) for each \(i\ge2\).
  2. Chain it by induction. \(\|\tilde b_i\|^2\ge 2^{-(i-1)}\|\tilde b_1\|^2=2^{-(i-1)}\|b_1\|^2\), using \(\tilde b_1=b_1\).
  3. Multiply the \(n\) inequalities. \((\det L)^2=\prod_i\|\tilde b_i\|^2\ge\prod_i 2^{-(i-1)}\|b_1\|^{2n}=2^{-n(n-1)/2}\|b_1\|^{2n}\).
  4. Take the \(2n\)-th root. \((\det L)^{1/n}\ge 2^{-(n-1)/4}\|b_1\|\), since \(\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2n}=\tfrac{n-1}{4}\). Rearranging gives \(\|b_1\|\le 2^{(n-1)/4}(\det L)^{1/n}\).
Traps. Integrality is the whole point: with real coefficients every lattice would contain arbitrarily short vectors, so say "\(\lambda_j\in\mathbb{Z}\), hence \(\lambda_j^2\ge1\)" explicitly. Do not claim the Gram–Schmidt vectors form a basis of the same lattice; they are orthogonal but generally not in \(L\). Keep track of squares: the reduction condition is on squared lengths, and the final bound takes a \(2n\)-th root, which is where the \(\tfrac{n-1}{4}\) comes from (\(\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\cdot\tfrac1{2n}\)). For true/false items, "orthogonal" is essential; the shortest-vector claim fails for a general basis.

Checklist

  1. Write the lattice vector as an integer combination and say why some coefficient has \(|\lambda_j|\ge1\).
  2. Use orthogonality (or Gram–Schmidt) to write a squared length as a sum or product of squared lengths.
  3. State the reduction condition you are using, with the correct constant \(\tfrac12\) on squared lengths.
  4. Chain by induction, multiply over \(i\), then take the right root; check the exponent \(\tfrac{n-1}{4}\).
  5. Conclude with the bound requested and note that LLL is polynomial time if runtime is asked.
