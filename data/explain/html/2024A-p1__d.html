Claim True: if every reduced cost in \(\tilde c_N\) is strictly positive at a basic feasible solution \(z\), then \(z\) is the unique optimum.

Strategy Write any feasible point's cost in terms of the reduced costs, then read off both optimality and uniqueness.

Key idea Moving away from \(z\) means turning on some nonbasic coordinate, and every such move strictly raises the cost because its reduced cost is positive.

Why this move Reduced costs are precisely the per-unit cost change of entering a nonbasic variable, so a positivity assumption on them is a direct handle on optimality.

  1. Value at \(z\). Since \(z_N=0\), the objective is \(c^\top z=c_B^\top A_B^{-1}b\).
  2. Value at any feasible \(y\). Expressing \(y\) through the basis gives \(c^\top y=c^\top z+\tilde c_N^\top y_N\), where \(\tilde c_N\) is the reduced-cost vector.
  3. Optimality. Feasibility forces \(y_N\ge 0\), and \(\tilde c_N>0\), so \(\tilde c_N^\top y_N\ge 0\) and \(c^\top y\ge c^\top z\).
  4. Uniqueness. If any coordinate of \(y_N\) were positive, then \(\tilde c_N^\top y_N>0\) and \(c^\top y>c^\top z\); so an optimum needs \(y_N=0\), and then \(Ay=b\) forces \(y_B=A_B^{-1}b=z_B\), i.e. \(y=z\).

Any step off \(z\) has to switch on a nonbasic variable, and each of those only makes the cost worse, so \(z\) stands alone at the bottom.
