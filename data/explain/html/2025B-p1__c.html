The statement is true, and it is a direct application of Markov's inequality. The key move is to turn a statement about students into a statement about a single random variable.

  1. Pick a student uniformly at random and let \(X\) be that student's grade. Because the student is uniform, for any threshold \(a\) the probability \(\Pr[X\ge a]\) equals exactly the fraction of students whose grade is at least \(a\). This is what lets us translate "at most half the students" into "probability at most \(1/2\)".
  2. The average grade of the class is precisely \(\mathbb{E}[X]\), the mean of \(X\) over the uniform choice of student.
  3. Grades are non-negative, so Markov's inequality applies: for a non-negative random variable and any \(a>0\), \(\Pr[X\ge a]\le \mathbb{E}[X]/a\).
  4. Apply it with \(a=2\mathbb{E}[X]\) (twice the average): \[\Pr\big[X\ge 2\,\mathbb{E}[X]\big]\le \frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{2\,\mathbb{E}[X]}=\frac12.\]
  5. By Step 1, the left-hand side is the fraction of students scoring at least twice the average. Hence at most half the class can achieve this.

(This implicitly assumes \(\mathbb{E}[X]>0\), i.e. the average is not zero; otherwise "twice the average" is \(0\) and the statement is trivial anyway. Markov gives the bound for free precisely because it needs nothing beyond non-negativity.)
