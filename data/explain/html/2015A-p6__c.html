Let \(x\) be the number of full bins and \(y\) the number of non-full bins the algorithm produces, and let \(1/k\) be the weight of the item that caused the last bin to be opened. We bound the total weight of all items from below. The \(x\) full bins contribute weight \(x\). By part (b), each of the other \(y-1\) non-full bins (all except the last-opened one) contributes at least \(1-\tfrac1k\). So \[ \text{total weight} \ge x + (1-\tfrac1k)(y-1) = x + y - 1 - \tfrac{y}{k} + \tfrac1k. \] By part (a) we have \(y\le k-1\), so \(\tfrac{y}{k}<1\), and therefore the total weight is greater than \(x+y-2\).

Every bin can hold total weight at most \(1\), so the optimal number of bins satisfies \(\mathrm{OPT}\ge \text{total weight} > x+y-2\). Since \(\mathrm{OPT}\) is an integer, \(\mathrm{OPT}\ge x+y-1\). The algorithm uses \(x+y\) bins, so it uses at most one bin more than the optimum. Hence the algorithm is an additive \(1\)-approximation.

Intuition. The items weigh nearly \(x+y-1\) in total, and since each bin holds at most \(1\), even a perfect packing needs almost that many bins, leaving the algorithm only one bin behind.
