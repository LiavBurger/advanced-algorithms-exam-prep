The statement is true. The quantity in question, \(\displaystyle\sum_{\substack{S\subseteq\{1,\dots,2n\}\\|S|=n}}\ \prod_{i\in S}\lambda_i\), is by definition the \(n\)-th elementary symmetric polynomial \(e_n(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{2n})\): the sum, over all size-\(n\) subsets, of the products of those eigenvalues. The key is that these symmetric functions of the eigenvalues appear directly as coefficients of the characteristic polynomial, which we can compute efficiently even though we cannot find the eigenvalues themselves.

  1. The characteristic polynomial of the \(2n\times 2n\) matrix \(A\) factors over its eigenvalues as \(p(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{2n}(x-\lambda_i)\).
  2. Expanding this product, the coefficient of \(x^{2n-k}\) equals \((-1)^k e_k(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{2n})\). This is exactly the relationship between roots and coefficients (Vieta's formulas).
  3. Taking \(k=n\): the coefficient of \(x^{n}\) is \((-1)^n e_n\), so \(e_n=(-1)^n\cdot[\text{coefficient of }x^n\text{ in }p(x)]\).
  4. In class we saw an algorithm that computes all coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of a given matrix in polynomial time (without computing eigenvalues). Read off the coefficient of \(x^n\) and multiply by \((-1)^n\).
  5. This yields \(e_n\), hence the desired sum, efficiently.
