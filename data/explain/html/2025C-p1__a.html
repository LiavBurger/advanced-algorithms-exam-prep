Claim True: if \(k\) divides \(n\), then every root of unity of order \(k\) is also a root of unity of order \(n\).

Strategy Write \(n=km\) and raise the defining equation \(\omega^k=1\) to the power \(m\).

Key idea "Order \(k\)" just means \(\omega^k=1\); divisibility lets you reach \(\omega^n=1\) by taking a power.

Why this move When a divisibility hypothesis meets an exponent, the natural move is to factor the big exponent through the small one so the known relation kicks in.

  1. Use the divisibility. Since \(k\mid n\), write \(n=km\) for some natural number \(m\).
  2. Start from the definition. Let \(\omega\) satisfy \(\omega^k=1\), i.e. \(\omega\) is a root of unity of order \(k\).
  3. Raise to the power. Then \(\omega^n=\omega^{km}=(\omega^k)^m=1^m=1\).
  4. Conclude. So \(\omega^n=1\), meaning \(\omega\) is a root of unity of order \(n\) too. Geometrically the \(k\) evenly spaced points sit among the \(n\) evenly spaced ones.
