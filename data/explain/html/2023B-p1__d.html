Claim True — such a constant \(C\) exists, by the spectral mixing of random walks.

Strategy Track the walk's distribution in the eigenbasis; the small second eigenvalue makes it converge to uniform in \(O(\log n)\) steps.

Key idea The uniform direction is fixed by the walk, and every other direction shrinks by at least \((1/2)\) each step, so after \(O(\log n)\) steps the distribution is essentially \(\tfrac1n\mathbf 1\).

Why this move A gap between the top eigenvalue and the rest is the signature of an expander, and the standard reach for "reaches everywhere fast" is the spectral mixing argument from class.

  1. Set up the walk. Let \(A\) be the normalized adjacency (transition) matrix; start at \(s\) with indicator vector \(v\). After \(k\) steps the distribution is \(A^k v\).
  2. Split into uniform plus remainder. Write \(v\) as its uniform component plus a part orthogonal to \(\mathbf 1\). Applying \(A^k\) keeps the uniform part (eigenvalue \(1\)) and shrinks the rest by at most \((1/2)^k\).
  3. Take \(k=C\log n\). Then \((1/2)^k\) is tiny, giving \(A^k v=\tfrac1n\mathbf 1+\boldsymbol\varepsilon\) with error norm below \(n^{-100}\).

The chance of being at \(t\) is the \(t\)-th entry, \(\tfrac1n+\varepsilon_t\ge \tfrac1n-n^{-100}\ge \tfrac1{2n}\). So the walk reaches \(t\) within \(C\log n\) steps with probability at least \(\tfrac1{2n}\).
