The statement is false. A nonnegative determinant is far weaker than positive semidefiniteness, and a single counterexample settles it.

  1. Take \(A=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\[2pt]0&-1\end{bmatrix}=-I\). It is symmetric (equal to its transpose), so it qualifies as a candidate for the statement.
  2. Its determinant is \(\det(A)=(-1)\cdot(-1)-0=1\ge 0\), so the hypothesis "\(\det(A)\ge 0\)" holds.
  3. But \(A\) is not PSD. Recall \(A\) is PSD iff \(x^\top A x\ge 0\) for all \(x\), equivalently iff all its eigenvalues are \(\ge 0\). Here \(A=-I\) has eigenvalue \(-1\) with multiplicity two, and \(x^\top(-I)x=-\lVert x\rVert^2<0\) for any \(x\ne 0\). So \(A\) is negative definite, the opposite of PSD.

Why the implication fails in general: \(\det(A)=\prod_i\lambda_i\) is only the product of the eigenvalues. An even number of negative eigenvalues (here two) makes the product positive while the matrix is nowhere near PSD. The determinant sees the parity of the negative eigenvalues, not whether any exist.
