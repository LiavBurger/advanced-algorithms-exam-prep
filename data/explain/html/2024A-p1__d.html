True. Let \(z\) be the basic feasible solution with basis \(B\), so \(z_N=0\) and its objective value is \(c^\top z=c_B^\top A_B^{-1}b\). Let \(y\) be any feasible solution, i.e. \(Ay=b\) and \(y\ge 0\). Writing \(y\) in terms of the basis, its cost is

\[c^\top y=c_B^\top A_B^{-1}b+\tilde c_N^\top y_N=c^\top z+\tilde c_N^\top y_N,\]

where \(\tilde c_N\) is the reduced-cost vector on the nonbasic variables.

Since \(y\) is feasible, \(y_N\ge 0\), and by assumption every entry of \(\tilde c_N\) is strictly positive, so \(\tilde c_N^\top y_N\ge 0\) and therefore \(c^\top y\ge c^\top z\); hence \(z\) is optimal. For uniqueness: if some coordinate of \(y_N\) were strictly positive, then \(\tilde c_N^\top y_N>0\) and \(c^\top y>c^\top z\), so \(y\) would not be optimal. Thus any optimal \(y\) has \(y_N=0\), and then \(Ay=b\) forces \(y_B=A_B^{-1}b=z_B\), so \(y=z\). Therefore \(z\) is the unique optimal solution.

Intuition. Any move off \(z\) has to switch on a nonbasic variable, and with positive reduced costs each such move only makes the cost worse.
