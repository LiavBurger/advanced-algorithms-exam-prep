The added constraints are the subtour-elimination constraints: for every proper subset \(Q\subsetneq V\),

\[\sum_{u\in Q}\ \sum_{v\in Q:(u,v)\in E} x_{u,v}\ \le\ |Q|-1,\]

i.e. the number of chosen edges with both endpoints inside \(Q\) is at most \(|Q|-1\).

Take any feasible solution. As in part (b), the degree constraints force the chosen edges to form a vertex-disjoint union of directed cycles that together cover all of \(V\). Suppose, for contradiction, that this is more than one cycle. Pick one of the cycles and let \(Q_0\) be its set of vertices; since at least one other cycle exists, \(Q_0\subsetneq V\) is a proper subset. That cycle uses exactly \(|Q_0|\) edges, all with both endpoints in \(Q_0\), so

\[\sum_{u\in Q_0}\ \sum_{v\in Q_0:(u,v)\in E} x_{u,v}\ =\ |Q_0|.\]

But the subtour constraint for \(Q_0\) requires this sum to be at most \(|Q_0|-1\), and \(|Q_0|>|Q_0|-1\), a contradiction. So no cycle can close on a proper subset of the vertices. The only remaining possibility is a single cycle through all vertices, that is, a Hamiltonian cycle. Together with part (a), the feasible solutions now correspond exactly to Hamiltonian cycles.

Intuition. A short loop inside a small set uses one edge too many for that set, so the new cap on internal edges bans every subtour.
