The statement is true. The key is that the number of perfect matchings of a bipartite graph is the permanent of its biadjacency matrix, and the permanent equals the determinant when we work modulo \(2\).

  1. Let the two sides of \(G\) have \(n\) vertices each, and let \(A\) be the \(n\times n\) matrix with \(A_{i,j}=1\) if left-vertex \(i\) is adjacent to right-vertex \(j\), and \(0\) otherwise. A perfect matching is a bijection \(\sigma\in S_n\) pairing left \(i\) with right \(\sigma(i)\) using only real edges, i.e. one with \(A_{i,\sigma(i)}=1\) for all \(i\). The product \(\prod_{i=1}^n A_{i,\sigma(i)}\) is \(1\) exactly when \(\sigma\) is such a matching and \(0\) otherwise, so the number of perfect matchings is \[\operatorname{perm}(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\prod_{i=1}^n A_{i,\sigma(i)}.\]
  2. The determinant is the same sum with signs: \[\det(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}(-1)^{\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)}\prod_{i=1}^n A_{i,\sigma(i)}.\]
  3. Modulo \(2\) the sign disappears, because \(-1\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\), so every term of \(\det(A)\) equals the corresponding term of \(\operatorname{perm}(A)\). Hence \(\det(A)\equiv\operatorname{perm}(A)\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\), i.e. \(\det(A)\bmod 2 = 1\) iff the number of perfect matchings is odd.

The determinant is computable in polynomial time (e.g. Gaussian elimination), so the algorithm just computes \(\det(A)\) and outputs \(\det(A)\bmod 2\). This decides the parity efficiently.
