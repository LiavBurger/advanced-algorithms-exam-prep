We need an instance where the LP relaxation (same constraints, but \(x_v\ge 0\) instead of \(x_v\in\{0,1\}\)) has optimum strictly below the integer optimum. The example is the \(4\)-cycle \(C_4\) with all weights \(1\); the fractional solution sets three vertices to \(\tfrac12\) and the fourth to \(0\).

Key idea: fractional \(x\) can "spread" domination across a vertex's closed neighborhood, satisfying \(\sum\ge 1\) more cheaply than any integral choice can.
  1. Setup. \(C_4\) has vertices on a square; each vertex has exactly \(2\) neighbors, so its closed neighborhood \(N(v)\cup\{v\}\) has \(3\) vertices. Assign one vertex \(x=0\) and the other three \(x=\tfrac12\).
  2. Feasibility. Check \(\sum_{u\in N(v)\cup\{v\}}x_u\ge 1\) for each \(v\). The zero vertex: its two neighbors are the two adjacent \(\tfrac12\) vertices, giving \(0+\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1\ge 1\). Each \(\tfrac12\) vertex: its closed neighborhood always contains at least two \(\tfrac12\) vertices, so its sum is \(\ge 1\). All constraints hold.
  3. LP cost. \(3\cdot\tfrac12+0=\tfrac32\). So the LP optimum is \(\le\tfrac32\).
  4. Integer cost. One chosen vertex dominates only itself plus its \(2\) neighbors = \(3\) of the \(4\) vertices, missing the opposite corner, so a single vertex is not enough; a minimum dominating set of \(C_4\) needs \(2\) vertices, cost \(2\).

Hence LP optimum \(=\tfrac32<2=\) ILP optimum: the relaxation is not integral (an integrality gap of \(\tfrac{4}{3}\)).
