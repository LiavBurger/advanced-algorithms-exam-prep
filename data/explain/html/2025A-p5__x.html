We test the polynomial identity \(\det(A_1)=\det(A_2)\) by evaluating both determinants at a random point (Schwartz–Zippel).

  1. For each variable \(x_{i,j}\), pick an independent uniformly random integer \(r_{i,j}\in\{1,\dots,100n\}\), and substitute \(x_{i,j}=r_{i,j}\) into both symbolic adjacency matrices.
  2. Compute the two numeric determinants \(\det(A_1)\) and \(\det(A_2)\), for instance by Gaussian elimination.
  3. Declare that \(G_1,G_2\) are determinantally equivalent if and only if the two numbers are equal.

The algorithm is efficient, since it only computes two \(n\times n\) determinants. For correctness, let \(A_i\) be the symbolic adjacency matrix of \(G_i\) and consider the polynomial \(P=\det(A_1)-\det(A_2)\) in the variables \(x_{i,j}\). A determinant is a sum of products of \(n\) entries, so \(\deg(P)\le n\).

If \(G_1,G_2\) are determinantally equivalent, then \(\det(A_1)\equiv\det(A_2)\) as polynomials, so \(P\equiv 0\); then for any choice of the \(r_{i,j}\) the two evaluations are equal and the algorithm answers correctly with no error. If they are not equivalent, then \(P\not\equiv 0\), and by the Schwartz–Zippel lemma the probability that a nonzero polynomial of degree at most \(n\) vanishes at a uniformly random point over a set of size \(100n\) is at most \(n/(100n)=1/100\). So the algorithm wrongly answers "equivalent" with probability at most \(1/100<0.1\), and otherwise correctly answers "not equivalent." The error probability is below \(0.1\).

Intuition. Two determinants are equal as polynomials exactly when their difference is the zero polynomial, and a nonzero low-degree polynomial almost never vanishes at a random point.
