The statement is false. The claim is that some nonzero polynomial of degree less than \(m\) vanishes at every point whose coordinates are order-\(m\) roots of unity. Schwartz–Zippel rules this out.

  1. Let \(\Omega_m\subseteq\mathbb{C}\) be the set of \(m\)-th roots of unity. There are exactly \(m\) of them, so \(|\Omega_m|=m\).
  2. Suppose \(f(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) is a nonzero polynomial with total degree \(\deg(f)
  3. The Schwartz–Zippel lemma bounds the chance a nonzero polynomial vanishes on a random point drawn from a finite set \(S\) per coordinate: \[\Pr_{a_1,\dots,a_n\in\Omega_m}\big[f(a_1,\dots,a_n)=0\big]\le \frac{\deg(f)}{|\Omega_m|}=\frac{\deg(f)}{m}<1,\] where the last inequality is exactly the hypothesis \(\deg(f)
  4. Since the probability of vanishing is strictly less than \(1\), the event "\(f\neq 0\)" has positive probability. A positive-probability event is non-empty, so there exists a point \((a_1,\dots,a_n)\in\Omega_m^{\,n}\) with \(f(a_1,\dots,a_n)\neq0\).

Therefore no nonzero polynomial of degree below \(m\) can vanish on all such points, contradicting the claim. The probabilistic argument is just a clean way to prove existence of a non-root.
