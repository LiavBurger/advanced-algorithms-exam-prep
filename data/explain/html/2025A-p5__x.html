Two directed graphs \(G_1,G_2\) are determinantally equivalent if the determinants of their symbolic adjacency matrices are identical as polynomials in the variables \(x_{i,j}\). We cannot expand these determinants symbolically (they can have exponentially many monomials), so we test the polynomial identity by evaluating at a random point—this is exactly polynomial identity testing via Schwartz–Zippel.

  1. Algorithm. For every variable \(x_{i,j}\), pick an independent uniformly random integer \(r_{i,j}\in\{1,\dots,100n\}\) and substitute \(x_{i,j}=r_{i,j}\). Evaluate both determinants numerically (each is just the determinant of a concrete number matrix, computable in polynomial time). Declare \(G_1,G_2\) determinantally equivalent iff the two numbers are equal.
  2. Setup for correctness. Let \(A_i\) be the symbolic adjacency matrix of \(G_i\), and define the polynomial \(P=\det(A_1)-\det(A_2)\). Every determinant of an \(n\times n\) matrix is a sum of products of \(n\) entries, so \(\deg(\det A_i)\le n\), hence \(\deg(P)\le n\).
  3. Equivalent case (no false negatives). If the graphs are equivalent then \(\det(A_1)\equiv\det(A_2)\), so \(P\equiv 0\). Then \(P\) evaluates to \(0\) for any choice of \(r_{i,j}\), the two determinants agree, and the algorithm correctly says "equivalent" with certainty.
  4. Non-equivalent case (bounded false positives). If they are not equivalent then \(P\not\equiv 0\). By the Schwartz–Zippel lemma, a nonzero polynomial of degree \(\le n\) evaluated at a random point over a set of size \(100n\) vanishes with probability at most \(\deg(P)/|S|\le n/(100n)=1/100\).
  5. So the algorithm errs only in the non-equivalent case, with probability at most \(1/100<0.1\). It runs in polynomial time (two determinant evaluations), meeting the requirement.
