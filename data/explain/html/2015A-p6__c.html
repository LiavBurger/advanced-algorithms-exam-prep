Goal Prove the algorithm uses at most one bin more than the optimum (an additive \(1\)-approximation).

Strategy Bound the total item weight from below using parts (a) and (b), then compare with how many bins OPT needs.

Key idea If the total weight is more than \(x+y-2\), then even a perfect packing needs at least \(x+y-1\) bins, while the algorithm uses \(x+y\).

Why this move Total weight is the universal lower bound on bins (each holds at most \(1\)), so counting weight is how you compare against OPT.

  1. Bound the weight. Let \(x\) be the full bins and \(y\) the non-full bins; \(1/k\) opened the last bin. Full bins give \(x\); by part (b) each of the other \(y-1\) non-full bins gives at least \(1-\tfrac1k\). Total weight \(\ge x+(1-\tfrac1k)(y-1)=x+y-1-\tfrac{y}{k}+\tfrac1k\).
  2. Use part (a). Part (a) gives \(y\le k-1\), so \(\tfrac{y}{k}<1\). Hence total weight \(> x+y-2\).
  3. Compare with OPT. Every bin holds at most weight \(1\), so \(\mathrm{OPT}\ge\text{total weight}>x+y-2\). Being an integer, \(\mathrm{OPT}\ge x+y-1\).

The algorithm uses \(x+y\) bins and \(\mathrm{OPT}\ge x+y-1\), so it uses at most one extra bin — an additive \(1\)-approximation.
