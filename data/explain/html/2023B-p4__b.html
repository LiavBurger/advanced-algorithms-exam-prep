Claim The converse fails — a feasible IP solution need not be a Hamiltonian cycle.

Strategy Exhibit a graph whose edges split into two disjoint cycles, each obeying the degree constraints.

Key idea The degree constraints only force in-degree and out-degree \(1\); they never force a single connected tour.

Why this move The hint points at connectivity — the fastest counterexample is a disconnected union of small cycles, which no degree constraint can rule out.

  1. Pick the graph. On six vertices take two vertex-disjoint directed triangles, \(1\to2\to3\to1\) and \(4\to5\to6\to4\); set \(x_{u,v}=1\) on those six edges, \(0\) elsewhere.
  2. Check feasibility. In each triangle every vertex has exactly one chosen incoming and one chosen outgoing edge, so every incoming and outgoing sum is \(1\) and all variables are \(0/1\). Feasible.
  3. Show it isn't Hamiltonian. A Hamiltonian cycle is one cycle through all vertices, but here the vertices split into two separate cycles — the subgraph isn't even connected.

This is the classic subtour phenomenon: the degree constraints allow any collection of disjoint cycles covering all vertices, which is exactly the gap part (c) fixes.
